Intro to College-Level Learning: Autonomy, Competence, and Relatedness

Understanding Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

College learning requires shifting from external compliance (doing work purely for grades, attendance, or parents) to self-directed intrinsic motivation. Self-Determination Theory, developed by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, demonstrates that deep learning and persistence happen when three basic psychological needs are satisfied.

The Three Core Psychological Needs

Autonomy

Ownership & Choice

The feeling of being the author of your own life and learning. Autonomy does not mean working completely alone; it means having choice, self-direction, and personal alignment with your tasks.

In College: Selecting paper topics that align with your career interests, setting your own daily study schedules, and choosing elective courses purposefully.

Saboteur: Feeling micromanaged or viewing class assignments as meaningless busywork forced upon you.

Competence

Mastery & Growth

The need to feel effective, capable, and skilled. Competence develops through tackling optimal challenges, mastering difficult material, and receiving constructive feedback.

In College: Breaking a 10-page research paper into manageable milestones, attending tutoring sessions, and tracking skill improvements over time.

Saboteur: Harsh unguided critiques, fixed mindset ("I'm just not a math person"), or overly simple/impossible tasks.

Relatedness

Belonging & Connection

The need to feel connected to others, valued by peers and faculty, and part of an academic community. Learning is fundamentally social and relational.

In College: Forming active study groups, attending professor office hours, participating in campus clubs, and supporting classmates.

Saboteur: Feeling isolated in massive lecture halls, imposter syndrome, or fear of asking "dumb" questions.

The Motivation Spectrum

Motivation is not all-or-nothing. It exists on a continuum from pure external pressure to complete internal satisfaction:

  1. Amotivation: Complete lack of intent or care. "I don't see why I bother going to class."
  2. External Regulation: Driven purely by external rewards or avoiding punishment. "I'm studying only so my parents don't cut off my allowance."
  3. Identified Regulation: Valuing the utility of the task, even if not enjoyable. "Statistics is hard, but mastering data analysis will make me a better psychologist."
  4. Intrinsic Motivation: Engaging in learning out of pure curiosity, interest, and inherent joy. "I lost track of time reading this astronomy article because it's fascinating!"
